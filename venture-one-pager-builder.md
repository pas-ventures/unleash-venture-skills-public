# Venture One-Pager Builder Skill

### At a glance

**Who it's for** — Founders who get one shot in front of an angel, operator or advisor to be interesting.

**What it does for you** — Reads your completed venture assessment and tells you what an investor would find missing. Then it builds a branded one-page PDF in your own CI, plus the intro message that carries it.

**When to use it** — Before any cold or referred approach to an angel, operator or advisor. Also whenever someone says "send me something short".

**Where it comes from** — The 11-section venture assessment used inside the Unleash community, compressed to the single page a busy reader will actually finish.

**How to use it** — Fill in the assessment first, then point this skill at it. It challenges you on what's missing, then pulls your brand colours off your website and builds the PDF and the blurb from there.

---

## Overview

This skill does three things, in order:

1. **Audits** your completed venture assessment against what an angel, operator or advisor needs to see — and tells you exactly what is thin or missing, in their words, not yours.
2. **Builds** a one-page PDF in your own brand — logo, colours, fonts pulled from your website — laid out as boxes a reader can scan in forty seconds.
3. **Writes** the intro blurb that goes with it: the email or DM that gets the one-pager opened.

The one-pager isn't trying to be a deck — its only job is to earn a reply.

## The prerequisite (do not skip this)

**This skill runs on a completed venture assessment.** Not a paragraph, not a deck, not "I'll explain it on the call".

If the founder has not completed one, stop and say so:

> Before we build this, work through the venture assessment — all 11 sections. It takes a few hours and that's the point: it's the thinking a one-pager compresses. Come back with it filled in and this takes twenty minutes.

The 11 sections are: 1 Company Snapshot · 2 Founders & Origin · 3 Customer & Problem · 4 Product & Value Proposition · 5 Market & Competitive Landscape · 6 Traction & Evidence of PMF · 7 Business Model & Economics · 8 GTM Plan to the next milestone · 9 Roadmap & Defensibility · 10 Fundraising & Use of Funds · 11 Biggest Risks & How We Can Help.

Any equivalent long-form business overview works as the input. What does not work is building the one-pager first — you end up with a designed page that says nothing.

## How to run this: the advisor in the room

Read the assessment like an investor deciding whether to take the meeting — someone who has run a company themselves and knows what real numbers look like. Vision and mission don't move that person. They are looking for two things and they find them fast:

**A trend that is going one way**, stated plainly and backed by something they can check. A specific change in the customer's world that is bigger this year than last and will be bigger again next year — not a market that is merely "growing" or a category that is "hot".

**Real numbers, stripped down to real progress.** What has actually happened, who actually paid, and how many of them are still there — not activity, and not what is planned.

Everything else on a first-touch page is decoration.

This skill's job is to make the founder produce four proofs, and to keep asking until they do or admit they can't:

| The proof | The question behind it | What makes it real |
|---|---|---|
| **The insight** | What do you know about this market that a smart outsider wouldn't? | A specific, growing trend plus the consequence nobody has costed. If a competent stranger could have guessed it, it isn't an insight |
| **Affluency** | Can these people actually pay? | Income, budget or existing spend — what they already pour into the workaround, in currency |
| **Willingness to pay** | Have they actually paid? | Money that changed hands. Free users who converted, a price held, a renewal taken. Interest, waitlists and "would definitely buy" are not this |
| **Conviction and conversion** | Do they stay, and does the group behave the same way? | Retention over time, frequency of use, depth per customer — and whether the second cohort did what the first one did |

Two of those four are about the *group* rather than the product, and founders under-answer them almost every time. If the segment can't pay, there is no market yet, whatever the pain looks like. If it can pay and won't convert, the product isn't proven yet either. The page has to show the group has money, buys and stays, or the reader has no reason to believe the market is there.

**Separate the founder's personal reason from the commercial case.** Many founders build from something personal, and they lead with it. It reads as conviction to the founder. To a reader deciding whether the segment has money and buys, it reads as noise. Keep it where it is a real advantage — access to a channel or a customer nobody else can reach — and state it as that advantage. It does not belong in the headline. The headline is the segment and its economics.

## What a great one-pager actually is

**It is a teaser for a first conversation.** The reader should finish it knowing the business is real, the market is worth the trip, and there's a specific reason to talk to *you*. They should still have two or three questions. Those questions are the meeting.

That framing decides what goes on the page. A teaser opens on the insight and withholds the round. A document that leads with "$1.2M on a SAFE at a $6M cap" has asked a stranger to price a deal they aren't yet interested in — and the reader starts doing valuation arithmetic instead of getting curious about the business.

**A reader gives it forty seconds and asks three things:**
1. Is this real? — traction, numbers, named customers
2. Is it big enough to matter? — the market slice, counted from the bottom up
3. Why this founder? — the edge that isn't copyable

**One page means one page** — not 8pt type and 4mm margins to force it. If it doesn't fit, the thinking isn't sharp enough yet.

**The three ways it fails:**
- **The feature dump** — what it does, in ascending detail, and nothing about who pays or why now
- **The fog** — "significant time savings", "huge market", "strong traction". These phrases tell an investor you haven't measured anything yet
- **The sprawl** — asking for money, an intro and a co-founder in the same paragraph. Pick one ask
- **The dashboard** — five or six operational metrics in a row, each needing a glossary. A reader who has to work out what "78% logo retention" or "30+ NPS" means has stopped reading about your business and started reading a report

**Hold something back on purpose.** Name the mechanism, don't explain it. "A sensor that flags a cold-chain break within four minutes" is enough — the reader who cares will ask how. Answer everything and the one-pager just gets filed.

**Open on the customer insight.** The strongest first line on any teaser is the one that makes the reader see the problem differently — usually a sentence the founder's own customers keep saying, quoted. It earns the next thirty seconds. "The operating system for X" does not: every reader has seen that sentence forty times and has no way to tell one from another.

## Why it starts with a trend and an insight

Use the skill and you get a page. The six ideas below are what let you write the next one yourself. None of this is new — it is direct-response advertising and behavioural psychology, applied to how investors actually read — and every claim has a source at the bottom.

### 1. You can't create demand — you can only point at demand that's already moving

Eugene Schwartz's argument in *Breakthrough Advertising* is that copy never creates desire. It takes hopes and fears that already exist in a market and channels them onto one product. Your one-pager can't manufacture an investor's interest in cold-chain losses. It can show them a force already in motion — a cost rising, or a rule landing — and put your business in its path.

This is why the macro trend goes first and the product goes fourth. A reader who meets your product before the trend has nothing to hang it on.

Schwartz's second idea matters as much: **stages of awareness**. Copy has to start where the reader's knowledge actually is. A cold angel is not "product aware" or even "problem aware" about your segment — they have never thought about it. Open with the shift in their world rather than your solution, because that is where the reader is standing.

### 2. The headline is most of the work, because most of the page never gets read.

Ogilvy's numbers: on average five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy, and once you have written the headline you have spent eighty cents of your dollar. Kennedy makes the same point structurally — the headline carries the message-to-market match, and everything after it is elaboration.

The evidence on reading behaviour is worse than most founders assume. Nielsen Norman Group put the ceiling at 28% of the words on an average page, with 20% the likelier figure. DocSend and Harvard's Tom Eisenmann tracked 200 startups who raised $360M and found investors spend an average of **3 minutes 44 seconds on an entire pitch deck** — about eleven seconds a slide.

A one-pager gets a fraction of that. Write the headline as though it is the only thing that will be read, because for most recipients it is.

### 3. What you show first decides how the rest gets read

Cialdini's *Pre-Suasion* is about the moment before the argument: what a communicator draws attention to first changes how everything afterwards is received. This is the whole reason the mission stays out of the lead.

Lead with segment economics and the reader evaluates the market. Lead with your personal story and they evaluate your motives instead — a different, harder question, and not one you'll win in forty seconds.

### 4. Curiosity runs out — hold something back so the meeting has a job to do

George Loewenstein's information-gap theory, published in *Psychological Bulletin* in 1994, defines curiosity as a deprivation state produced by the perception of a gap between what you know and what you want to know. The finding that matters for a one-pager: a small amount of information acts as a **priming dose** that sharply increases curiosity, while consuming enough of it produces satiation and curiosity drops away.

Explain the whole business and the meeting has no job left to do — the reader knows enough. Name the mechanism, leave the how unexplained, and the meeting has one. The Heath brothers make the same argument in *Made to Stick*: open a gap in someone's knowledge and withhold the close.

### 5. Exact numbers read as measured. Round numbers read as guessed

Claude Hopkins in *Scientific Advertising*, over a century ago: generalities don't register with a reader, and definite figures do. It is still the cheapest credibility available to a founder.

"$38,000 a year" reads as something you measured. "Tens of thousands a year" reads as something you guessed. Same with a sample size: "1,900 pharmacies surveyed" sounds like a real study happened, and "a large survey" sounds like one might have. The Heaths call this concreteness, and it is what lets a reader repeat your claim to a partner without sounding vague.

### 6. Big numbers anchor, which is why the sized funnel starts wide and ends on a dollar figure

Kahneman and Tversky's anchoring effect: the first number a person sees sets the frame for every judgement that follows. Open on four million households and every number after it is read against a real market. Open on nine paying customers and everything after is read against nine.

Anchoring is not permission to inflate. An anchor the reader can check builds the frame. One they can't takes the rest of the page down with it, which is why the sized funnel insists on a bottom-up, sourced number at the top.

### And why investors specifically read for trend and insight

Three ideas from the venture side explain why this structure fits the reader you are writing to.

**Markets decide outcomes.** Marc Andreessen's "The Only Thing That Matters" argues that market is the dominant factor in a startup's fate, ahead of team or product. Sequoia's own business-plan guidance requires a **"Why now?"** section. Put the market and the timing in your first two lines — for most investors that is the only part that gets read.

**The insight has to be contrarian to be worth anything.** Peter Thiel's question in *Zero to One* — what important truth do very few people agree with you on? — is the test your insight has to pass. If a smart outsider could have guessed it, it's context, and context earns you nothing.

**The one-pager's job is to start a relationship, not close a deal.** Mark Suster's "Invest in Lines, Not Dots" makes the case that investors back a trajectory, not a single meeting. That is the argument for asking for fifteen minutes instead of a cheque: this page turns a stranger into the first dot.

## The copy engine

The page has one job at a time: **stop the reader, earn the next thirty seconds, get the coffee.** A founder who works through this section can write their own headline without a copywriter in the room.

### Hook → Story → Offer

| Beat | On the page | What it has to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | The headline and the line beneath it | Stop a reader who was about to archive the email. One idea, one cost, no company description |
| **Story** | The short block under the headline | What the founder saw that others hadn't. How they went and checked it. What happened when they charged money |
| **Proof** | Two or three numbers | Kill the doubt the story just created. One number for the pain, one for people paying, one for the size of it |
| **Vision** | Where this goes next | Give the reader something to be excited about that isn't this quarter |
| **Offer** | Who I'm looking for, then the coffee | Ask for something small enough that saying yes costs nothing, from a named kind of person |

### Three doubts a reader never says out loud

An investor or advisor reading a cold one-pager holds all three of these and voices none of them. The page has to answer each one or it gets filed:

| Doubt | Sounds like | Answered by |
|---|---|---|
| **The timing isn't special** | "People have had this problem forever" | The external force (Gate 9), in the hook and story |
| **They won't actually pay** | "Nice product, no business" | The buy number — people who chose to pay, with the base and the benchmark |
| **This founder can't be the one** | "Interesting, wrong team" | The epiphany plus what has already shipped. Not a CV — evidence they saw it early and then went and built it |

If you can't point at the line on the page that answers each one, the page isn't finished.

### Six headline patterns

Pick one and test it before you write anything else. These are the shapes that work for a business one-pager, and a founder does not need to invent a new one.

| Pattern | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The sized funnel** *(start here)* | {How many of them there are}. {What that segment does, in volume}. {What it costs them a year, in currency} | "Sixty thousand independent pharmacies still reconcile insurance claims by hand. The ones we sell to work through 400 rejected claims a month, and write off $38,000 a year they were owed" |
| **The reversal** | The better a {customer} gets at {good thing}, the more {scarce resource} it costs them | "The more locations a franchise opens, the more of one operator's week goes to fixing payroll" |
| **The uncounted cost** | {Trend everyone celebrates} created {a bill}. {Named person} has been paying it | "Every dental practice in the country went digital. The receptionist still types the chart notes at 9pm" |
| **The institutional gap** | {Institutions} keep {a record}. {The people they serve} keep {nothing} | "Every wholesaler they buy from keeps a record of the order. Nobody keeps a record of the shop" |
| **The insider count** | We {specific research on N people}. {Surprising thing} happened {almost every time} | "We watched 40 distributors load a truck. Six of them checked the temperature" |
| **The already-paying claim** | {Customer} spends {exact amount} a year solving this badly, and nobody has sold them a better way | "R576,000 a year walks out of a mid-size distributor's cold room, and nobody has ever sold them a way to stop it" |
| **The deadline** | {Named change} lands in {month}, and {customer} has no way to meet it | "POPIA's breach-reporting deadline lands in March, and not one of our nine pilot customers can produce the file" |

**Try the sized funnel first.** It is the pattern that works most often, because it answers all three of the reader's forty-second questions in one breath: is this real, is it big enough, and who pays. Three facts, each narrower than the last, landing on money.

Three rules make it work, and it collapses without them:
1. **The big number is counted bottom-up and sourced.** A downloaded market total at the top of the page undoes everything below it
2. **The middle fact is a behaviour, not an attitude.** "Works through 400 rejected claims a month" is checkable. "Cares deeply about compliance" is not
3. **The last number is per customer, per year, in currency.** It is the one the reader repeats to someone else

A reversal headline earns its abstraction only if the line directly beneath it carries the number. "The more locations a franchise opens, the more of one operator's week goes to fixing payroll" works only when the next line says *eleven hours a week, at twelve sites*. Without that, it is a poster.

**Test the headline before anything else gets written:**
1. **Could a competitor put their name on it?** If yes, it's a category claim. Rewrite
2. **Does it contain something the reader can picture, and a cost they can feel?** Abstractions don't hook
3. **Read it to someone outside the business.** If they can't say what's interesting about it without reading further, it isn't a hook yet

### Specificity: the cheapest credibility there is

Round numbers read as guesses. Exact numbers read as measurements, and measurements read as a founder who knows their business.

- **Use the exact figure.** $38,000. 1,900 pharmacies surveyed. 12 to 20 rejected claims a week. A round number reads as a guess
- Give the base with every percentage — "14% of the 210 trials we ran, against a ~3% category median"
- Name the source inside the sentence. A reader will not chase a footnote, and an uncited number reads as invented
- One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs a semicolon to hold together, it's two sentences

### The offer: ask for the smallest yes

The one-pager is not selling the round. It is selling fifteen minutes.

- **Name the person.** "Two or three people who have sold software into independent pharmacy groups" lets a reader decide in one second whether it is them. "Angels and advisors" makes them decide it probably isn't
- **Make the meeting small and concrete.** Fifteen minutes over coffee gets said yes to more often than "a call" or "a meeting" does. It is a unit of time nobody has to protect
- **Promise a specific artifact.** "I'll show you the funnel, including the part that isn't working" gives them a reason to show up and signals you'll be straight with them. A meeting to "tell you more about the business" promises a pitch
- **One honest weakness, stated as the interesting problem.** It's the line that separates a founder from a brochure
- **No round terms.** See Step 5

## Step-by-step procedure

### Step 1 — Read the assessment and map it

Read all 11 sections before writing anything. Then map each assessment answer onto the one-pager blocks:

| One-pager block | Comes from |
|---|---|
| Headline + one-liner | 1.1, 1.2 |
| Stage chip | 1.3 |
| The problem, in money | 3.2, 3.3, 3.5 |
| How it's handled today | 3.4, 3.6 |
| What you built + the value | 4.1, 4.3, 4.4 |
| Traction | 6.1, 6.2, 6.4 |
| Market, counted bottom-up | 5.2, 5.5, 7.2 |
| Next six months | 8.1, 10.3 |
| The ask | 1.4, 10.1, 11.2 |
| Why this team | 2.2, 2.3 |

Never invent a number to fill a box. A missing number is a question for the founder, not a gap to write around.

### Step 2 — Run their numbers before you score anything

Open a calculator and check the assessment's own arithmetic before the audit. This takes five minutes and it is the highest-value thing in the whole skill — a reader who spots a number that doesn't reconcile stops reading, and founders rarely catch these themselves because they wrote each section on a different day.

Check at least:

- **The market multiplication.** Does the stated segment size × the stated contract value equal the figure they claim?
- **ARR versus revenue booked in a year.** These are different numbers and they get used interchangeably. Retained accounts × annual price is ARR. Everyone billed during the year × price is bookings. If the plan says "£X ARR by December" and the model says "£X revenue in that year", ask which one it is — it is often a 2× difference
- **Any percentage against its base.** "35% of accounts add a second seat at £60" has to reconcile with the revenue line it supposedly produces
- **The same metric stated twice.** Current ARPU, ACV, customer count and price appear in several sections. When two sections disagree, even slightly, flag it — an investor reading front to back will see both

Report what doesn't reconcile as a question with the arithmetic shown, never as an accusation:

> **Section 8.1 says "£400K ARR by December"; section 8.5 shows £400,000 of revenue booked during that year, ending at 1,100 retained accounts.** Those are different claims. 1,100 accounts on a £240 annual plan is about £264K of ARR, and the £400K is everyone billed across the whole year. Which one is the milestone? The bookings figure is the bigger number, and it is the one an investor will discount.

### Step 2b — The readiness audit (the challenge pass)

Score the four proofs first. They decide whether anyone takes the meeting. Each is READY, THIN or MISSING:

| # | Proof | What "READY" means |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **The insight** | A named, sourced trend moving in one direction, plus the consequence the founder found that nobody has counted. It survives the outsider test: a smart stranger could not have guessed it. |
| P2 | **Affluency of the group** | Evidence these people have money against this problem — income, budget line, or what they already spend on the workaround, in currency per year. |
| P3 | **Willingness to pay** | Money that actually changed hands, with the base and a benchmark. Free-to-paid conversion, a price held, a renewal. Waitlists and stated intent are MISSING, not THIN. |
| P4 | **Conviction and conversion** | They came back and they went deep — retention over a stated period, frequency, depth per customer — and ideally the second cohort behaved like the first. |

Then five supporting gates, which shape the page but rarely decide the meeting:

| # | Gate | What "READY" means |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | **The one-liner** | What you do, for whom, what outcome, in a sentence a stranger repeats back correctly. |
| S2 | **The status quo and why it breaks** | The named tool, spreadsheet, agency or manual process they use today — and the specific point at which it fails them. |
| S3 | **Market, bottom-up** | Number of customers in the market you start in × realistic annual contract value. Never a top-down analyst figure. |
| S4 | **The ask, known** | The founder can state the amount and instrument, or the exact skill and time commitment, without hesitating. This tests that they know it — the first-touch page will not print it (see Step 5). |
| S5 | **Next six months** | Three milestones with dates. What the reader's money or time buys. |

**On P1, be strict about what counts as the trend.** Founders almost always answer "why now" with an investor-side statistic — a technology-adoption number, a capital-flow figure, a CAGR — rather than a customer-side one. Those explain why an investor might be interested this year. They don't explain why the customer's world got harder.

What counts is a force acting on the customer — costs rising faster than revenue, a regulation landing, a behaviour that has genuinely shifted. Pick the one that is real for this founder and name it. The test is whether it makes the problem measurably worse each year for the person who would pay them.

If the assessment doesn't have one, that is a real MISSING — and it is often the most valuable question you can put to a founder, because the answer usually exists in their head and has never been written down or sourced. Ask for it, and ask what would evidence it.

**On P3 and P4, refuse the substitutes.** Founders reach for the nearest available number when the real one is small or unflattering: signups instead of subscribers, installs instead of conversions, "strong engagement" instead of a retention figure with a period attached. None of those is a proof, and swapping one in costs more credibility than the small real number would have.

Present the two tables, then write the gap register underneath. For every THIN or MISSING item: name the section, quote the line, and ask the question that fixes it.

**How to give this feedback:** name the gap and ask the question. Don't rewrite it for them, and frame every gap as what the reader still needs to see.

> **P2 — THIN.** Section 3.5 reads "saves them a lot of admin time". An angel cannot price that, and it doesn't tell them whether these people have money. How many hours a week, at what loaded cost per hour — or what did one customer write off last year because of it?

> **S3 — MISSING.** Section 5.2 gives a $4.1bn global market. That figure tells the reader you haven't counted anything yourself. How many businesses in your first province or country actually look like your best customers, and what would each pay you a year?

Three to eight gaps is normal on a first pass. Fewer than three usually means the audit wasn't hard enough.

### Step 3 — Ask, then wait

Put the open questions in one numbered block — never more than eight — and stop. Do not build the page around placeholders and do not proceed on assumptions. If the founder genuinely cannot answer a gate, that is a finding: say plainly that the one-pager will be weaker at that box and why the reader will notice.

### Step 4 — Take the brand off their website

Ask for the website URL and fetch it. Pull:

| Input | How to get it |
|---|---|
| `{LOGO}` | Ask for a PNG or SVG. Fall back to the wordmark set in the heading font — never a generic icon |
| `{PRIMARY_HEX}` | Dominant brand colour from the site's CSS or buttons |
| `{INK_HEX}` | Body text colour, usually near-black |
| `{MUTED_HEX}` | Secondary text, usually a mid grey |
| `{RULE_HEX}` | Hairline / border colour |
| `{HEADING_FONT}` | Heading typeface, with a websafe fallback |
| `{BODY_FONT}` | Body typeface, with a websafe fallback |

Show the founder the extracted hex values and fonts and get a yes before rendering. If there's no website yet, offer one restrained default — dark ink on off-white with a single accent colour — and say it's a placeholder until they have a brand.

Print rules that override brand: white or off-white page background, accent used for rules, labels and one number strip only. Do not reproduce a dark website background on a page people will print and scribble on.

### Step 5 — Write the page

Work the copy engine above as you write this: hook, story, proof, vision, offer, with the three beliefs each broken by a named line.

**The insight is a trend, not a complaint.** The strongest opening line names a shift the reader hasn't priced yet, and lands the consequence nobody has counted. The shape is: *the world changed in this specific way → here is the cost that came with it that nobody costed → here is who is carrying it, and they will pay to put it down.* A customer quote is excellent evidence for that consequence, but on its own it is a complaint, and complaints have been around forever. The trend is what makes a reader think the timing is now.

**Never let the page understate the stage.** If there is a shipped product with paying customers, that fact belongs in the first third — in the status line at the top, and again in the first number. A hero built purely on customer research reads as pre-product, and a founder who has already shipped, charged and retained will be read as earlier than she is. Interviews prove you understood the problem; a paid subscriber proves somebody agreed. Lead with whichever is further along.

**Open on the insight about the customer, not on what you do.** The first thing on the page is the sentence that makes a stranger think "I hadn't looked at it that way." Best case it is a verbatim line from the founder's own research — the phrase their customers keep saying, in the customer's words. Never open with a category claim ("the operating system for X", "the leading platform for Y"): a reader has seen forty of those and can't tell them apart. The company name and the category chip sit small at the top; the insight is the headline.

Under the insight, one short paragraph that turns it into a market: who has this problem, why it is getting worse, and why the people who have it can pay.

**Then two or three heavy numbers. Not five, and not a metrics dashboard.** Each one has to land on a reader who knows nothing about the business:

- **Prefer currency.** What the problem costs a customer a year, what the market is worth, what a customer is worth. Money needs no explanation
- **Kill any number that needs a glossary.** NPS, "D30 retention", activation rate, "78% logo retention" — these mean something internally and nothing to a first-time reader. If a number would make them ask "what does that mean?", it is costing you, not helping you
- **Use the ratio when the ratio is the story.** "Converted at five times the category median" hits harder than "14% conversion", and it survives being read quickly
- **Caption each number in one plain sentence** saying why it matters. The number is the hook; the caption is the proof

**Then two short columns:** what you built, in the language a customer would use, and where it goes — how the thing compounds. That second column is what makes an operator lean in.

**Then the reason for writing, and one CTA.**

| Block | Contains | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| **Top line** | Company name · category · geography · stage. Small and quiet | 8 words |
| **The insight** | The customer's own words, or the sentence that reframes the problem | 15 words |
| **The thesis** | Insight → market. Who, why now, why they can pay | 50 words |
| **Two or three numbers** | Currency where possible, each with a one-sentence caption | 3 figures |
| **What we built** | Plain words. One line of evidence that people use it hard | 60 words |
| **Where it goes** | What compounds — why this gets more valuable and harder to leave | 55 words |
| **Why I'm writing** | The honest state of play in two sentences, then one CTA | 60 words |
| **Footer** | Founder name · role · email · LinkedIn | one line |

**Leave the round out of it.** A first-touch teaser does not carry the amount, the instrument, the valuation, the cap, dilution, or the use of funds. Those are the second conversation, and printing them invites a reader to price the deal before they are interested in the business. The ask on the page is a conversation:

> I'm looking for two or three people who have {specific experience}. If that's you: fifteen minutes over coffee, and I'll show you {the specific thing}, including the part that isn't working yet.

The founder still has to *know* their round cold — Gate 7 tests that they do. The page just doesn't say it.

**Copy rules for every block:**
- Currency beats percentages; percentages beat adjectives. A sentence with no number and no proper noun is a candidate for cutting
- Every number gets a plain-English caption. A number a reader can't interpret is worse than no number
- Name real customers and real tools, or say "under NDA" — never "a leading retailer"
- Banned: revolutionary, disrupting, seamless, cutting-edge, game-changing, "AI-powered" as the whole value proposition, "we are passionate about"
- One honest line about what isn't working yet, framed as the interesting problem rather than a confession. It is the single strongest trust signal on the page, and it is what stops a teaser reading as a brochure
- One CTA, first person, and the smallest possible yes — "fifteen minutes over coffee", with the thing you will show them named. Not "let's connect"

**The team line.** If the founding team's track record is the strongest asset, it goes as one quiet line under the insight or in the footer. Never a whole box.

**What to leave out on purpose.** Feature lists, the roadmap, the tech stack, competitor grids, the cap table, the round, and every operational metric that needs a glossary. If the page answers everything, there is no reason to take the call. Aim to leave the reader with two questions they have to ask you.

**If the founder wants a longer version** — a milestone block with three dated commitments for the next six months fits under the two columns, and turns the teaser into a follow-up document for someone who has already replied. Keep it off the first-touch version.

### Step 6 — Render the PDF

Emit an HTML file and render it with headless Chrome or Puppeteer. Rules that keep it to one page:

1. CSS owns the paper. `@page { size: A4; margin: 14mm 13mm; }` and `preferCSSPageSize: true`. Do not also pass a margin option to the renderer — the two fight and the page silently becomes two
2. `page-break-inside: avoid` on every box, the number strip and the footer
3. Body text at 9.5pt minimum. If it overflows, cut words — never shrink below 9.5pt or drop the margins
4. Write the HTML next to the PDF so the founder can tweak without re-rendering
5. If the page comes out less than two-thirds full, do not stretch the type. Add the two blocks that earn the space: a one-line team credential under the one-liner, and one verbatim customer sentence under the traction strip. Empty space at the bottom of a one-pager reads as thin, not as confident
6. Verify the output is one page before showing it. If your environment has `pymupdf`: `python3 -c "import fitz; print(fitz.open('one-pager.pdf').page_count)"` — anything other than 1 is a fail, and the fix is copy, not layout

### Step 7 — Write the intro blurb

One blurb. It works for an angel, an operator or an advisor because the difference between them is two lines, not a different letter.

**Five beats, 110 words or fewer:**

1. **The bridge** — how you got to them. A referral name, or the specific thing of theirs you read, built on, or bought. Never "I came across your profile"
2. **What you are** — one line, with the number in it
3. **The proof** — one traction fact, and only one
4. **Why them** — the named skill or experience they have, pointed at the specific gap you have. Most founders skip this line, and it's the one that actually gets replies
5. **The soft ask** — their read on it. Money is the second conversation, even when money is the actual point

**Subject line:** four to seven words, concrete, no question mark. "Cold-chain sensors — 9 SA distributors" beats "Quick question about your experience".

**Template:**

> Subject: {CONCRETE_SUBJECT}
>
> Hi {NAME},
>
> {BRIDGE — "{referrer} suggested I write to you" / "Your piece on X was the reason we changed Y"}.
>
> I'm building {COMPANY} — {ONE_LINE_WITH_A_NUMBER}. {ONE_PROOF_POINT}.
>
> I'm reaching out to you specifically because of {THEIR_NAMED_EXPERIENCE} — that's exactly the part I'm weakest on right now, {YOUR_NAMED_GAP}.
>
> One-pager attached. If it's interesting: fifteen minutes over coffee, and I'll show you {the specific thing} — including the part that isn't working yet.
>
> {FOUNDER_NAME}

**The two lines that change by reader:**

| Reader | Beat 4 becomes | Beat 5 becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Angel** | why their portfolio or sector view makes this relevant to them | "I'm raising {AMOUNT} on a {INSTRUMENT} and would rather start with your read than a pitch" |
| **Operator / advisor** | the specific skill you want, and the specific hours: "two calls a month" | "I'd love your opinion first, and to see if an advisory arrangement makes sense after" |
| **Design partner / customer** | why their business looks like the ones already using it | "20 minutes to show you what the first nine are seeing" |

**Blurb rules:**
- Never attach a deck to a first email. The one-pager is the attachment
- No "I hope this finds you well", no "quick question", no "just following up on my previous email" in the first message
- Never ask for money and advice in the same message
- Say what you want in one sentence, and make the next step something they can do in a calendar

### Step 8 — Self-check before handing it over

Run every item. Any fail is a rewrite, not a note.

- [ ] The PDF is one page
- [ ] Two or three headline numbers, no more, and a reader outside the business can interpret every one of them without asking
- [ ] The first line is an insight about the customer, not a description of the company
- [ ] The insight names an external force with a source behind it, not just a customer complaint
- [ ] The page does not read as earlier-stage than the business actually is. Shipped, charging and retaining all appear above the fold if they are true
- [ ] No round size, valuation, instrument or cap anywhere on the page
- [ ] Every claim traces back to a section of the assessment
- [ ] The market figure is a multiplication the reader can check, not a downloaded total
- [ ] Every headline number on the page reconciles with the assessment's own model — you checked, you didn't assume
- [ ] Where a metric is small (a handful of paying users), the denominator is on the page next to the percentage. A percentage with a hidden base is the fastest way to lose a reader who was on your side
- [ ] Exactly one ask, in one sentence, and it asks for a conversation
- [ ] One honest line about what isn't working yet
- [ ] The CTA is the smallest yes — fifteen minutes, with a named artifact you'll show them
- [ ] "Who I'm looking for" is narrow enough that a reader knows in one second whether it means them
- [ ] You can point at the line that answers each of the three doubts: timing, willingness to pay, and this founder
- [ ] All four proofs are on the page: the insight, that the group has money, that they paid, and that they stayed
- [ ] No proof has been substituted with an activity number — signups standing in for subscribers, installs for conversions, "strong engagement" for a retention figure with a period attached
- [ ] The mission, if there is one, appears as a commercial advantage or not at all
- [ ] The headline passes all three headline tests, including: a competitor could not put their name on it
- [ ] No acronym or internal metric appears without expansion on first use
- [ ] The page leaves the reader with at least two questions they have to ask you
- [ ] The brand colours and fonts match their website — confirmed with the founder, not guessed
- [ ] Read the blurb aloud. Anywhere your voice flattens, cut it
- [ ] Run the whole thing through [stop-slop](https://github.com/hardikpandya/stop-slop) before it leaves their hands

## Worked example (fictional)

*FreshLink is invented, including every number, to show the shape of the output.*

### The input, abbreviated

> **3.5 What the problem costs them today** — "Distributors lose a lot of stock when the chain breaks and it's a real headache for them."
> **5.2 Market size** — "Cold chain logistics is a $9.2bn market globally growing at 12% CAGR."
> **6.1 Traction** — "We have a few paying customers and good feedback so far."

### The audit that follows

| The four proofs | Status |
|---|---|
| P1 The insight | **MISSING** |
| P2 Affluency of the group | **THIN** |
| P3 Willingness to pay | **THIN** |
| P4 Conviction and conversion | **THIN** |

| Supporting | Status |
|---|---|
| S1 One-liner | READY |
| S2 Status quo | READY |
| S3 Market bottom-up | **MISSING** |
| S4 The ask, known | **MISSING** |
| S5 Six months | READY |

> **P1 — MISSING.** Nothing anywhere says why this is a 2026 business rather than a 2019 one. Cold chains have been breaking for decades. What has changed for your customer — fuel, electricity, stock costs, a retailer's compliance requirement, load-shedding — that makes a warm load cost more this year than it did three years ago? Find that and it becomes the first line on the page.
>
> **P2 — THIN.** 3.5 says stock loss is "a real headache". An angel prices headaches at zero, and it tells them nothing about whether these distributors have money. What did one write off last year in rand, and what do they already spend trying to prevent it?
>
> **P3 — THIN.** "A few paying customers" won't survive one follow-up question. How many, at what monthly value, and did anyone pay a second time?
>
> **P4 — THIN.** Nothing here says whether they stayed. How many vehicles are live today versus at signup, how often does a depot open the dashboard, and did your second group of customers behave like your first?
>
> **S3 — MISSING.** 5.2 gives a $9.2bn global figure. An angel skips straight past a line like that — it says nothing about you. How many independent distributors in South Africa run 5–40 refrigerated vehicles, and what would each pay you a year?
>
> **S4 — MISSING.** Nothing in section 10 or 11.2 says what you want. Are you raising, or are you looking for an advisor who has sold into food distribution before? The page won't print the amount either way, but you can't write the closing line until you know which one it is.

### The founder's answers, and the page they produce

**Top line** — FreshLink · Cold-chain · South Africa · Seed

**The insight** — *"We only find out the truck got warm when the customer opens the door."* — said by six of the eleven distributors we interviewed

**The thesis** — Every independent frozen-food distributor in the country runs on a clipboard and a driver's word. Fuel and stock costs have risen faster than their margins for three years running, so the write-off that used to be annoying is now the difference between a good month and a bad one.

**The numbers**
- **R576,000** — what one distributor writes off in a year in spoiled stock. They are already paying for this problem, to nobody
- **62%** — how far that fell across our first nine customers, from R48,000 a month to R18,000
- **R132m** — the 1,400 SA distributors running 5–40 refrigerated vehicles, at our R94,000 average contract. 430 are in Gauteng, where we start

**What we built** — A cabin sensor and a driver app that tells the depot a load is going warm while there is still time to do something about it. Nine distributors run 143 vehicles on it and none has left.

**Where it goes** — Every trip logged builds a temperature record per vehicle, per route, per driver. That record is what an insurer prices off and what a national retailer demands from its suppliers — and it only exists if you were there from the first load.

**Why I'm writing** — The product works and the customers stay. What we haven't proven is that we can sell it faster than word of mouth. *I'm looking for two or three people who have sold into national food retail. Fifteen minutes over coffee, and I'll show you the live temperature dashboard from three customers.*

### The blurb

> Subject: Cold-chain sensors — 9 SA distributors live
>
> Hi Nomsa,
>
> Lerato Dube suggested I write to you.
>
> I'm building FreshLink — we catch a cold-chain temperature break in four minutes instead of at the delivery door. Nine distributors are live on 143 vehicles, and their monthly write-offs have gone from R48k to R18k.
>
> I'm writing to you specifically because you built the retail distribution side at a national cold-chain group — that's exactly where I'm weakest. I can sell to owner-operators; I have no idea how to get through a national buying desk.
>
> One-pager attached. If it's interesting, I'd value 20 minutes of your read on it — and if an advisory arrangement makes sense after that, even better.
>
> Thabo
